# Evidence-Bound Scientific Writing Principles

## Accuracy before fluency

The accountable human authors are responsible for accuracy, integrity, originality,
attribution, and disclosure, including material prepared with AI assistance
[SW-S01, SW-S03]. Do not improve prose by changing scientific meaning.

Apply these rules:

- Preserve the distinction between observation, estimate, interpretation, and
  speculation.
- Match the strength of each verb to the design and evidence.
- Do not turn association into causation.
- Do not turn statistical non-significance into equivalence or proof of no effect.
- Report uncertainty with the estimate and keep its interpretation proportional.
- Preserve conflicting evidence and credible alternative explanations.
- State what is unknown instead of filling a gap.

## Confirmatory and exploratory work

Label analyses according to their actual provenance.

- **Confirmatory**: prespecified before the relevant analysis or unblinding threshold,
  with deviations recorded.
- **Exploratory**: generated or materially changed after examining relevant data.
- **Descriptive**: summarizes observed data without a confirmatory inferential claim.

Do not relabel a post hoc analysis as prespecified. Explain amendments, timing, and
rationale. Keep exploratory results useful but visibly exploratory.

## Complete reporting

Retain findings regardless of direction:

- primary and secondary outcomes;
- negative, null, adverse, and unexpected findings;
- missing data and attrition;
- sensitivity and subgroup analyses with their status;
- protocol or analysis-plan deviations;
- failed or inconclusive experiments when relevant to interpretation.

Selective omission can distort the record. Corrections should be prompt, transparent,
and linked to the affected version [SW-S01, SW-S27].

## Numbers and units

Every reported number needs:

- a stable concept name;
- unit and scale;
- numerator and denominator when applicable;
- analysis population and sample size;
- time point;
- estimate and uncertainty where applicable;
- method or result ID;
- evidence ID.

Keep precision justified by measurement and analysis. Distinguish zero from missing,
below detection, not measured, and not applicable. Recheck affected numeric facts and
method-result mappings after substantive changes; run the consistency checker when
those records change and for final readiness.

## Methods and results

Methods describe what was actually done, not what would have been ideal. Results must
not introduce an undeclared method. For each result, verify:

- the outcome was defined;
- the analysis method exists in the methods registry;
- the analysis intent agrees;
- exclusions and analysis populations agree;
- transformations, covariates, multiplicity handling, and missing-data methods agree;
- the reported value, unit, denominator, sample size, and uncertainty agree everywhere.

## Limitations and generalizability

Name concrete sources of bias, imprecision, missingness, measurement error, model
limitations, multiplicity, and limited transportability. Explain likely direction or
consequence when evidence supports that explanation. Do not add a generic limitations
paragraph merely to satisfy form.

Place a condition or uncertainty beside the claim it limits, explaining its consequence
when needed. Merge repeated qualifications; retain distinctions that change the
interpretation, such as the evaluated population, measurement boundary, or a failed
criterion. Keep review status and verification history in the established draft markers
and review records rather than repeating the audit process in manuscript paragraphs.

## Argument and paragraph development

- Organize a passage around the judgment or explanation the reader needs. Supply the
  relevant definition, reasoning, or evidence and explain its implication; use the parts
  needed for that passage rather than enforcing a fixed paragraph template.
- State why a method addresses the identified difficulty. Introduce the question and
  useful intuition before formal assumptions or derivations, without weakening them.
- In related work, connect methods by the technical distinction relevant to the claim.
  Avoid a sequence of paper summaries that leaves the comparison to the reader.
- Establish the task, comparison, metric, and sampling unit before relying on internal
  experiment labels. Distinguish reported findings, their interpretation, and protocol
  details through their placement; give each enough context to be understood.

## Manuscript voice convention

As a local author preference, avoid first-person manuscript prose where possible
(`I`, `we`, `our`, `我`, `我们`, and equivalents). Prefer concrete subjects such as the
method, controller, analysis, bound, experiment, or result. This is not a universal IEEE
or scientific-writing requirement.

First person may be used when explicitly naming a concept or method that the authors
themselves name, for example, "We call this rule ..." or "我们将该规则命名为……".
Authorship of a method alone does not make every description of it a naming act.
Do not invent a name or originality claim to justify first person. Avoid mechanical
passivization, vague "it is shown" constructions, or ambiguous attribution; choose a
specific subject and preserve who performed or established a result when that matters.

## Language review

- Keep the grammatical subject and its main verb close enough to identify the action.
  Express operations with precise verbs instead of stacking abstract nouns.
- Connect new information to a term or question already established. Replace an
  ambiguous pronoun with its referent; do not ban pronouns that have clear antecedents.
- Define unfamiliar terms and symbols before relying on them. Keep one term for one
  concept, and explain when similar labels refer to different objects or populations.
- Retain sentences that add reasoning, evidence, or useful navigation. Merge restated
  claims and process narration that do not advance the passage.

Prefer direct, precise prose, but do not impose arbitrary sentence length, citation
density, recency percentage, figure count, reference count, abstract sentence count, or
contribution-list size. Those heuristics vary by field and venue. Do not ban accurate
technical verbs such as "combine" or "extend" to make the work sound more novel.

Use person-centered or identity-affirming language according to community preference,
study context, and current venue policy. Preserve participant self-description when
appropriate. Define abbreviations and use one term for one concept.

Judge revisions by whether the reader can follow the point, its support, and its scope,
while the scientific claims, evidence links, and mathematical meaning remain intact.

## Draft status

Bullets, notes, and placeholders are acceptable in an internal outline. They are not
submission-ready. Final structure may include lists when the target venue or content
benefits from them; there is no universal rule that every scientific section must be
continuous prose.
